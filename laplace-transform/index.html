Teaching aid for σ = 0: time-averaged matching

This extra curve is not the Laplace transform. It divides the running real-channel accumulation by elapsed time, A(t) = (1/t)∫0t f(τ)cos(ωτ)dτ, so repeated positive/negative cancellation becomes easier to see.

Two different ideas meet at s = σ + jω

σ = 0: finite-window Fourier-like view

e−st = e−jωt

There is no exponential damping. For an undamped sinusoid, the ordinary improper integral to ∞ does not generally converge. Over a finite window, however, frequency matching and cancellation are easy to visualize. Orthogonality is captured cleanly by the time-averaged teaching aid.

σ > 0: Laplace convergence

e−st = e−σte−jωt

The factor e−σt suppresses late-time contributions. For signals of suitable exponential order, the running integral approaches a fixed complex value as T grows.

Example: pure oscillation
ℒ{cos(ω0t)} = s / (s² + ω0²),   Re(s) > 0

A pure sinusoid therefore has a perfectly valid Laplace transform. The convergence comes from the positive real part σ of s—not from taking a time average.

Laplace transform
F(s) = ∫0∞ f(t)e−st dt,   s = σ + jω
Engineering payoff: this transform is not only a way to represent signals. For linear dynamic systems it turns derivatives into algebraic terms, for example ℒ{ẋ(t)} = sX(s) − x(0).
